## Why verification matters

A wrong education story can push a family to book travel for the wrong date, a student to prepare for an exam at the wrong time, or a parent to pay for a service that does not exist. The harm falls not only on the person who published it, but on everyone who believed it and acted on it.

Verification is not suspicion of everything. It is a short check before sharing.

## Check one: who published it?

Ask whether the source is a responsible body, an anonymous account, or a page that copies someone else.

- **Official source:** a ministry, academy or institution website, or a signed announcement.
- **Media source:** it may report the story, but it is not necessarily the body that announced it.
- **Anonymous source:** a page or account with no clear identity.
- **Repost:** someone passing on another person's claim without a link.

If you cannot find the original source, treat the story as unconfirmed.

## Check two: when was it published?

An old story can return every year and look new. Check:

- The publication date of the original article.
- The school year named in the text.
- The date of the image, if you can find it.
- Whether the body has issued a newer update.

A story about the start of the school year may be from a previous year. Comparing years is essential.

## Check three: is the link official?

Look at the domain. Official ministry sites normally use domains ending in `men.gov.ma`. Be careful with:

- Look-alike domains with an extra letter.
- Short links that hide the destination.
- Pages that imitate the official site's design.
- Links that ask for login details before showing a story.

If the link does not open or redirects to advertising, stop.

## Check four: does the text say what the claim says?

Many rumours are built on a true fragment. Read the whole text:

- Does it describe a **proposal** or a **decision**?
- Does it name a specific school year?
- Does it cite a source, or offer an interpretation?
- Are there phrases such as "informed sources" with no evidence?
- Is the image connected to the text, or just decoration?

Do not stop at the headline. A headline can mislead even in an accurate article.

## Check five: do the details converge?

Look for the official source or two independent sources. If the claim exists only on one unknown page, it is unconfirmed. If the official body has said nothing, do not attribute the story to it.

> [!WARNING] Do not share copied screenshots
> A screenshot can be edited easily. Even if it looks official, look for the original link. If you cannot verify it, say only that the story is unconfirmed.

## Common warning signs

- "Urgent" with an image and no source.
- A request to share "so everyone sees it" before any verification.
- A link asking for a password or phone number.
- A story threatening that "the account will be deleted" unless you log in now.
- A paid service offered alongside the story.
- A date or figure that does not match the school year.

## What to do instead of sharing

1. Keep the official link if you find one.
2. Write a calm correction if you shared something wrong.
3. Report the page or platform if the content is fraudulent.
4. Send the question to the school or the competent body.
5. Teach others the verification method rather than blaming them.

## Summary

Check the source, the date, the link, the text and whether details converge. Do not publish an education story that has no reliable origin. If the story concerns an official procedure, the official source decides, not the number of shares.
